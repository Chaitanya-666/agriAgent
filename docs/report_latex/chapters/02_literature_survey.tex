\chapter{Literature Survey}\label{ch:survey}

This chapter reviews 25 papers in detail, in the department's format (publication details,
introduction, proposed solution, results, gap and relevance), followed by the background works on
which they build and a summary table. The papers are ordered from the closest work outwards:
zero-shot crop--weed segmentation and the foundation models we use
(Papers~1--5), supervised crop--weed segmentation and its datasets (Papers~6--11), methods that use
the planting row instead of labels (Papers~12--13), weed management, spraying and vegetation indices (Papers~14--17),
weed classification and detection (Papers~18--23) and zero-shot classifiers and vision-language
models for weeds (Papers~24--25). Every paper's details were checked against its DOI record, and its summary is
based on the paper's own abstract and text.

% ─────────────────────────────── Closest work and foundation models
\paper{Zero-Shot Semantic Segmentation for Robots in Agriculture}
{Y. L. Chong, L. Nunes, F. Magistri, X. Zhong, J. Behley, C. Stachniss}
{IEEE/RSJ Int.\ Conf.\ Intelligent Robots and Systems (IROS), 2025, pp.~5432--5439}
{10.1109/IROS60139.2025.11246227}
\pp{Introduction} Crop--weed segmentation for weeding robots is normally fully supervised and needs
expensive pixel labels for every new field. The authors ask whether it can be done without labels~\cite{chong2025zeroshot}.
\pp{Proposed solution} The method (``Weeds are Weird'', WaW) treats weeds as anomalies: weeds are
rarer and more varied than the crop. Every plant segment is described in the feature space of a
pretrained foundation model (BioCLIP~\cite{stevens2024bioclip}); the crop is modelled as a set of
hyperspheres around a bag of crop features, and any plant outside that manifold is called a weed.
\pp{Results} Zero-shot crop--weed segmentation works on several real field datasets, with a mean
weed IoU of 12.1\% (11.1\% on PhenoBench) against 62.7\% for a supervised ERFNet, and 9.1\% when
generic CLIP features replace BioCLIP.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Weed IoU is far below supervised training. The authors also argue that
text-prompted pipelines such as Grounded SAM are ill-defined for crop and weed, but do not measure
that pipeline on the task.
\pp{Relevance} This is the closest peer-reviewed work and our comparison point. AgriAgent measures
the text-prompted pipeline they criticise (our RQ1) and adds a safety layer on top.

\paperarxiv{Grounded SAM: Assembling Open-World Models for Diverse Visual Tasks}
{T. Ren, S. Liu, A. Zeng, J. Lin, K. Li, H. Cao et al.\ (17 authors)}
{arXiv technical report 2401.14159, 2024}
{https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.14159}
\pp{Introduction} Open-world perception needs models that both find objects named in free text and
outline them precisely; no single model did both well~\cite{ren2024groundedsam}.
\pp{Proposed solution} Grounded SAM chains an open-set detector (Grounding DINO) with a promptable
segmenter (SAM): the detector's boxes become the segmenter's prompts, and each mask inherits the
text label of its box. Further models can be added for tagging, editing or tracking.
\pp{Results} The combination reports 48.7 mean AP on the zero-shot SegInW benchmark.
\pp{Gap / limitation} It is evaluated on general images; nothing is reported on agricultural
scenes, where crop and weed are both ``a green plant''.
\pp{Relevance} \textbf{This is the pipeline AgriAgent implements}, with SAM~2 in place of SAM.

\paper{Grounding DINO: Marrying DINO with Grounded Pre-training for Open-Set Object Detection}
{S. Liu, Z. Zeng, T. Ren, F. Li, H. Zhang, J. Yang et al.\ (12 authors)}
{European Conf.\ Computer Vision (ECCV 2024), LNCS, pp.~38--55}
{10.1007/978-3-031-72970-6_3}
\pp{Introduction} Closed-set detectors only find the classes they were trained on. Open-set
detection should find any object named by a category name or a referring expression~\cite{liu2024groundingdino}.
\pp{Proposed solution} The transformer detector DINO is fused with language at three stages: a
feature enhancer, language-guided query selection and a cross-modality decoder, trained with
grounded (image--text) pre-training.
\pp{Results} 52.5 AP on COCO zero-shot transfer (no COCO training data) and a mean 26.1 AP on the
ODinW zero-shot benchmark.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Its labels are only as good as the text prompt; fine-grained classes that
look alike are not separated by a word.
\pp{Relevance} Our detector (the tiny variant). Our results show its ``weed'' and ``crop'' labels
are not reliable evidence on field photos (Section~\ref{sec:baseline}).

\paper{Segment Anything}
{A. Kirillov, E. Mintun, N. Ravi, H. Mao, C. Rolland, L. Gustafson et al.\ (12 authors)}
{IEEE/CVF Int.\ Conf.\ Computer Vision (ICCV), 2023, pp.~3992--4003}
{10.1109/ICCV51070.2023.00371}
\pp{Introduction} Segmentation models are usually trained per task and per dataset. The authors
propose a promptable segmentation task, a model and a dataset for it~\cite{kirillov2023sam}.
\pp{Proposed solution} The Segment Anything Model (SAM) produces a mask for any object given a
prompt (point, box or mask). It was trained with a data engine that built SA-1B, over one billion
masks on 11 million images.
\pp{Results} Zero-shot performance on many segmentation tasks is often competitive with, or better
than, fully supervised results.
\pp{Gap / limitation} SAM outlines objects but assigns no class, so it cannot by itself tell a
crop from a weed.
\pp{Relevance} We use its successor SAM~2~\cite{ravi2025sam2}; our measurements confirm that the
masks are good and the class labels are the problem.

\paper{BioCLIP: A Vision Foundation Model for the Tree of Life}
{S. Stevens, J. Wu, M. J. Thompson, E. G. Campolongo, C. H. Song, D. E. Carlyn et al.}
{IEEE/CVF Conf.\ Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition (CVPR), 2024, pp.~19412--19424}
{10.1109/CVPR52733.2024.01836}
\pp{Introduction} Biological image analysis uses bespoke models per task. A general vision model
for organisms is missing~\cite{stevens2024bioclip}.
\pp{Proposed solution} The authors build TreeOfLife-10M, a dataset of plant, animal and fungus
images, and train BioCLIP on it together with the structured taxonomy of the tree of life.
\pp{Results} BioCLIP outperforms existing baselines on fine-grained biology classification by
16--17\% absolute and learns a representation that follows the taxonomic hierarchy.
\pp{Gap / limitation} It classifies whole images; it does not locate plants in a field photo.
\pp{Relevance} The species-aware features used by WaW~\cite{chong2025zeroshot}; a natural
replacement for text labels in our future work (Chapter~\ref{ch:future}).

% ─────────────────────────────── Supervised crop–weed segmentation and datasets
\paper{Real-Time Semantic Segmentation of Crop and Weed for Precision Agriculture Robots Leveraging Background Knowledge in CNNs}
{A. Milioto, P. Lottes, C. Stachniss}
{IEEE Int.\ Conf.\ Robotics and Automation (ICRA), 2018, pp.~2229--2235}
{10.1109/ICRA.2018.8460962}
\pp{Introduction} Weeding robots must separate sugar beet, weed and soil in real time, ideally from
an ordinary RGB camera~\cite{milioto2018realtime}.
\pp{Proposed solution} A CNN whose input is extended with existing vegetation indices computed from
RGB (``background knowledge''), designed to run in real time and to be re-trained for new fields
with little data.
\pp{Results} Runs at about 20~Hz on a field robot in Germany and Switzerland. From RGB alone the
weed IoU is 20.6\%; with 14 input channels (RGB, near-infrared and indices) it is 59.2\%.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Supervised: each new field still needs labelled images.
\pp{Relevance} A supervised reference for sugar beet; like it, our rules use a vegetation index.

\paper{Fully Convolutional Networks With Sequential Information for Robust Crop and Weed Detection in Precision Farming}
{P. Lottes, J. Behley, A. Milioto, C. Stachniss}
{IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters, vol.~3, no.~4, pp.~2870--2877, 2018}
{10.1109/LRA.2018.2846289}
\pp{Introduction} Fields differ in weed pressure, weed types, growth stage and soil, and
classifiers trained on one field often fail on another~\cite{lottes2018fcn}.
\pp{Proposed solution} An encoder--decoder fully convolutional network that also looks at image
sequences along the row, so that it learns the spatial arrangement of the crop.
\pp{Results} The system generalises to previously unseen fields under varying conditions and
substantially improves crop--weed accuracy over other methods without re-training.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Needs pixel-labelled training data and image sequences from a robot.
\pp{Relevance} Evidence that the planting row is a strong cue; our rules 2 and 4 use the row
within a single photo, without training.

\paper{Robust Joint Stem Detection and Crop-Weed Classification Using Image Sequences for Plant-Specific Treatment in Precision Farming}
{P. Lottes, J. Behley, N. Chebrolu, A. Milioto, C. Stachniss}
{Journal of Field Robotics, vol.~37, no.~1, pp.~20--34, 2020}
{10.1002/rob.21901}
\pp{Introduction} Different weeds need different treatments: some only respond to mechanical
removal, others to selective spraying~\cite{lottes2020stem}.
\pp{Proposed solution} One end-to-end fully convolutional network estimates the stem positions of
plants (for mechanical tools) and the pixel area of crop and weed (for spraying), using image
sequences of field strips to exploit the crop arrangement.
\pp{Results} Tested on images from several farms in different countries; it generalises to unseen
fields and improves both stem detection and segmentation over earlier methods.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Supervised and robot-specific.
\pp{Relevance} Shows what a full treatment pipeline needs from vision; our output (spray zones and
GeoJSON) corresponds to its spraying branch.

\paper{Towards Domain Generalization in Crop and Weed Segmentation for Precision Farming Robots}
{J. Weyler, T. L\"abe, F. Magistri, J. Behley, C. Stachniss}
{IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters, vol.~8, no.~6, pp.~3310--3317, 2023}
{10.1109/LRA.2023.3262417}
\pp{Introduction} CNNs trained on one dataset (the source domain) often generalise poorly to unseen
fields (target domains)~\cite{weyler2023domain}.
\pp{Proposed solution} A two-step framework that uses unlabelled images from many fields: sparse
annotations are computed automatically (among others from fields sown in rows), and a style transfer
renders source images in the style of other fields.
\pp{Results} Better crop--weed segmentation across various fields than the state of the art.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Still trains a network on labelled source data.
\pp{Relevance} Documents gap G1 (supervised models do not transfer) and again uses crop rows to
obtain labels automatically.

\paper{PhenoBench: A Large Dataset and Benchmarks for Semantic Image Interpretation in the Agricultural Domain}
{J. Weyler, F. Magistri, E. Marks, Y. L. Chong, M. Sodano, G. Roggiolani et al.}
{IEEE Trans.\ Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence, vol.~46, no.~12, pp.~9583--9594, 2024}
{10.1109/TPAMI.2024.3419548}
\pp{Introduction} Large benchmarks drive progress in other domains, but agricultural datasets with
dense labels are rare~\cite{weyler2024phenobench}.
\pp{Proposed solution} A UAV-recorded dataset of real fields with pixel-wise crop and weed labels
and crop leaf instances, plus benchmarks on a hidden test set that includes a completely unseen
field.
\pp{Results} Baselines for several tasks, including semantic segmentation, are provided.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Sugar beet only; drone viewpoint.
\pp{Relevance} WaW reports 11.1\% weed IoU on it; running our pipeline on its validation set is our
first future-work item, for a like-for-like comparison.

\paper{Agricultural Robot Dataset for Plant Classification, Localization and Mapping on Sugar Beet Fields}
{N. Chebrolu, P. Lottes, A. Schaefer, W. Winterhalter, W. Burgard, C. Stachniss}
{Int.\ Journal of Robotics Research, vol.~36, no.~10, pp.~1045--1052, 2017}
{10.1177/0278364917720510}
\pp{Introduction} Agricultural datasets are hard to obtain because fields must be maintained and
recording must follow the growth season~\cite{chebrolu2017sugarbeet}.
\pp{Proposed solution} A field robot recorded a sugar beet farm near Bonn three times a week for
three months in 2016 with a multispectral camera, an RGB-D sensor, lidar, GPS and wheel encoders.
\pp{Results} A large public dataset covering the growth stages relevant for weed control.
\pp{Gap / limitation} One crop, one farm, one season.
\pp{Relevance} The sugar beet photos we evaluate on resemble this dataset.

% ─────────────────────────────── Row-based methods without labels
\paper{Deep Learning with Unsupervised Data Labeling for Weed Detection in Line Crops in UAV Images}
{M. D. Bah, A. Hafiane, R. Canals}
{Remote Sensing, vol.~10, no.~11, Art.~no.~1690, 2018}
{10.3390/rs10111690}
\pp{Introduction} Pixel-level labelling of agricultural images by experts is very slow, but deep
learning needs training data~\cite{bah2018unsupervised}.
\pp{Proposed solution} Crop rows are detected automatically in UAV images; vegetation between the
rows is taken as weed and used as an automatically labelled training set for a CNN that then
detects crop and weed.
\pp{Results} Accuracy comparable to manual labelling, within 1.5\% in a spinach field and 6\% in a
bean field.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Weeds inside the row are learned only indirectly; needs clear rows.
\pp{Relevance} The same idea as our rule 4 (off-row means weed), used here to create labels.
Our finding F1 shows its risk when the crop is not recognised.

\paper{Unsupervised Classification Algorithm for Early Weed Detection in Row-Crops by Combining Spatial and Spectral Information}
{M. Louargant, G. Jones, R. Faroux, J.-N. Paoli, T. Maillot, C. G\'ee, S. Villette}
{Remote Sensing, vol.~10, no.~5, Art.~no.~761, 2018}
{10.3390/rs10050761}
\pp{Introduction} Site-specific weed management needs weed detection that works without manual
data selection~\cite{louargant2018rowcrops}.
\pp{Proposed solution} The row arrangement first separates some crop and weed pixels by location;
these pixels then train a support vector machine on four-band multispectral features, which
classifies the remaining in-row pixels.
\pp{Results} On 14 images of maize and sugar beet, the mean weed detection rate was 89\% for the
combined method, 79\% for the spatial method alone and 75\% for the spectral method alone.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Needs wide rows, high resolution (at least 6~mm per pixel) and a
multispectral camera.
\pp{Relevance} Confirms that row position is a strong but incomplete cue; our size rule plays the
role of their second, in-row step.

% ─────────────────────────────── Weed management and spraying
\paper{Development and Evaluation of a Low-Cost and Smart Technology for Precision Weed Management Utilizing Artificial Intelligence}
{V. Partel, S. C. Kakarla, Y. Ampatzidis}
{Computers and Electronics in Agriculture, vol.~157, pp.~339--350, 2019}
{10.1016/j.compag.2018.12.048}
\pp{Introduction} Uniform spraying wastes chemicals, raises costs and risks crop damage, although
weeds are patchy~\cite{partel2019sprayer}.
\pp{Proposed solution} A smart sprayer with machine vision and a deep detector that separates target
weeds from non-targets such as vegetable crops and sprays only the targets; weed maps are produced
with RTK GPS.
\pp{Results} With real plants (portulaca as target; sedge and pepper as non-targets) the more
powerful GPU reached 71\% precision and 78\% recall, and the embedded Jetson TX2 59\% and 44\%.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Vision accuracy on real plants limits the system; the detector needs training.
\pp{Relevance} Shows that the nozzle side is cheap and the vision decision is the bottleneck,
which motivates our safety gate.

\paper{Advances in Site-Specific Weed Management in Agriculture---A Review}
{R. Gerhards, D. And\'ujar Sanchez, P. Hamouz, G. G. Peteinatos, S. Christensen, C. Fernandez-Quintanilla}
{Weed Research, vol.~62, no.~2, pp.~123--133, 2022}
{10.1111/wre.12526}
\pp{Introduction} Information and automation technology allow weed control to follow the spatial
and temporal patchiness of weeds~\cite{gerhards2022sswm}.
\pp{Proposed solution} A review of site-specific weed management: sensors (3D cameras,
multispectral imaging), AI for weed classification, decision algorithms and actuators.
\pp{Results} The review evaluates the ecological and economic benefits and the outlook for
practical farming.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Reliable automatic weed classification remains a precondition for adoption.
\pp{Relevance} Frames our problem: herbicide saving is only real if the classification is right.

\paper{Autonomous Robotic Weed Control Systems: A Review}
{D. C. Slaughter, D. K. Giles, D. Downey}
{Computers and Electronics in Agriculture, vol.~61, no.~1, pp.~63--78, 2008}
{10.1016/j.compag.2007.05.008}
\pp{Introduction} Robotic weed control promised to reduce herbicide use and manual weeding~\cite{slaughter2008review}.
\pp{Proposed solution} The review covers the parts of a weeding robot: row guidance, weed detection
and identification, precise in-row treatment and mapping.
\pp{Results} Guidance and actuation had matured; robust weed detection and identification under
field conditions had not.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Pre-dates deep learning; the identification problem it names is still open.
\pp{Relevance} The original statement of the problem AgriAgent works on.

\paper{Color Indices for Weed Identification Under Various Soil, Residue, and Lighting Conditions}
{D. M. Woebbecke, G. E. Meyer, K. Von Bargen, D. A. Mortensen}
{Transactions of the ASAE, vol.~38, no.~1, pp.~259--269, 1995}
{10.13031/2013.27838}
\pp{Introduction} Spot-spraying sensors must first separate living plants from soil and crop
residue~\cite{woebbecke1995color}.
\pp{Proposed solution} Several chromatic-coordinate indices were tested on images of weeds over
different soils and residues: $r-g$, $g-b$, $(g-b)/|r-g|$, $2g-r-b$ and a modified hue.
\pp{Results} The modified hue, the $2g-r-b$ (excess-green) index and the green chromatic coordinate
separated weeds from background best, in both sunlit and shaded conditions; the hue was the most
expensive to compute.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Separates plant from soil, not crop from weed.
\pp{Relevance} Our vegetation mask is exactly the excess-green index with an automatic threshold
(Section~\ref{sec:math-veg}).

% ─────────────────────────────── Weed classification and detection
\paper{A Review on Weed Detection Using Ground-Based Machine Vision and Image Processing Techniques}
{A. Wang, W. Zhang, X. Wei}
{Computers and Electronics in Agriculture, vol.~158, pp.~226--240, 2019}
{10.1016/j.compag.2019.02.005}
\pp{Introduction} Machine vision can give real-time weed information for site-specific weed
management~\cite{wang2019review}.
\pp{Proposed solution} A review of the four stages of weed detection: pre-processing, vegetation
segmentation (colour-index, threshold-based and learning-based), feature extraction (morphology,
spectra, texture, spatial context) and classification by classical and deep learning.
\pp{Results} The hardest step is telling crop from weed, which often have similar properties.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Occlusion and overlapping leaves, varying light and different growth stages
remain open challenges.
\pp{Relevance} Our plant rules use two of its feature families: morphology (size) and spatial
context (the row).

\paper{Plant Species Classification Using Deep Convolutional Neural Network}
{M. Dyrmann, H. Karstoft, H. S. Midtiby}
{Biosystems Engineering, vol.~151, pp.~72--80, 2016}
{10.1016/j.biosystemseng.2016.08.024}
\pp{Introduction} Site-specific weed management needs to know which weed species are present~\cite{dyrmann2016species}.
\pp{Proposed solution} A CNN trained from scratch on 10,413 images of 22 weed and crop species at
early growth stages, drawn from six datasets with different lighting, resolution and soil.
\pp{Results} 86.2\% classification accuracy across the 22 species.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Classifies single-plant images; needs labelled examples of every species.
\pp{Relevance} A supervised species-level reference; our system only separates crop from weed.

\paper{DeepWeeds: A Multiclass Weed Species Image Dataset for Deep Learning}
{A. Olsen, D. A. Konovalov, B. Philippa, P. Ridd, J. C. Wood, J. Johns et al.}
{Scientific Reports, vol.~9, no.~1, Art.~no.~2058, 2019}
{10.1038/s41598-018-38343-3}
\pp{Introduction} Robust classification of weed species in their natural environment is a major
obstacle to robotic weed control~\cite{olsen2019deepweeds}.
\pp{Proposed solution} A public dataset of 17,509 labelled images of eight weed species from eight
locations in northern Australia, with Inception-v3 and ResNet-50 baselines.
\pp{Results} 95.1\% and 95.7\% average accuracy; ResNet-50 runs in 53.4~ms per image.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Rangeland weeds, image-level labels, no crop class.
\pp{Relevance} A standard pretraining source for weed classifiers (used by Paper~24).

\paper{CNN Feature Based Graph Convolutional Network for Weed and Crop Recognition in Smart Farming}
{H. Jiang, C. Zhang, Y. Qiao, Z. Zhang, W. Zhang, C. Song}
{Computers and Electronics in Agriculture, vol.~174, Art.~no.~105450, 2020}
{10.1016/j.compag.2020.105450}
\pp{Introduction} Accurate weed recognition is needed for site-specific control, but labelled data
are limited~\cite{jiang2020gcn}.
\pp{Proposed solution} CNN features of images become nodes of a graph connected by feature
distance; a graph convolutional network propagates labels from labelled to unlabelled images
(semi-supervised).
\pp{Results} 97.80\%, 99.37\%, 98.93\% and 96.51\% accuracy on four weed datasets, better than
AlexNet, VGG16 and ResNet-101, at real-time speed.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Still needs some labels from each dataset.
\pp{Relevance} Another route to fewer labels; ours uses none.

\paper{Performance Evaluation of Deep Learning Object Detectors for Weed Detection for Cotton}
{A. Rahman, Y. Lu, H. Wang}
{Smart Agricultural Technology, vol.~3, Art.~no.~100126, 2023}
{10.1016/j.atech.2022.100126}
\pp{Introduction} Herbicide-resistant weeds call for treatments that target individual plants,
which needs robust weed detection under variable field conditions~\cite{rahman2023cotton}.
\pp{Proposed solution} The CottonWeedDet3 dataset (848 colour images from cotton fields, three weed
classes with boxes) and 13 detectors (YOLOv5, RetinaNet, EfficientDet, Fast and Faster R-CNN) trained
by transfer learning.
\pp{Results} RetinaNet (R101-FPN) reached the best 79.98\% mAP@0.5; YOLOv5n reached 76.58\% with
1.8 million parameters and 17~ms inference; augmentation added up to 4.2\%.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Supervised; cotton itself is not labelled.
\pp{Relevance} \textbf{The source of our cotton photos.} Its mAP is the trained ceiling against which
zero-shot results on cotton should be read.

\paper{YOLOWeeds: A Novel Benchmark of YOLO Object Detectors for Multi-Class Weed Detection in Cotton Production Systems}
{F. Dang, D. Chen, Y. Lu, Z. Li}
{Computers and Electronics in Agriculture, vol.~205, Art.~no.~107655, 2023}
{10.1016/j.compag.2023.107655}
\pp{Introduction} Weed detection for cotton needs a large, multi-class benchmark~\cite{dang2023yoloweeds}.
\pp{Proposed solution} The CottonWeedDet12 dataset of weeds in cotton fields with 12 weed classes,
and a benchmark of 25 YOLO detectors from YOLOv3 to YOLOv7.
\pp{Results} A reference table of accuracy and speed for each detector on cotton weeds.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Supervised; every detector is trained on the benchmark's labels.
\pp{Relevance} Together with Paper~22, shows how much labelled data supervised cotton detection
uses, which AgriAgent avoids.

% ─────────────────────────────── VLMs and zero-shot classification
\paper{Transfer and Zero-Shot Learning for Scalable Weed Detection and Classification in UAV Images}
{N. Belissent, J. M. Pe\~na, G. A. Mes\'ias-Ruiz, J. Shawe-Taylor, M. P\'erez-Ortiz}
{Knowledge-Based Systems, vol.~292, Art.~no.~111586, 2024}
{10.1016/j.knosys.2024.111586}
\pp{Introduction} Climate change may bring new or invasive weeds that a fixed classifier has never
seen~\cite{belissent2024transfer}.
\pp{Proposed solution} Transfer learning with ResNets pretrained on ImageNet and/or DeepWeeds,
fine-tuned on their new TomatoWeeds dataset, and zero-shot learning with image embeddings and
text descriptions of weed morphology and habitat.
\pp{Results} A ResNet50 pretrained on both datasets and fine-tuned on TomatoWeeds reached 77.8\%
holdout accuracy; zero-shot recognition of unseen classes was still far from satisfactory.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Zero-shot weed classification is not yet usable.
\pp{Relevance} Independent evidence for our RQ1 answer.

\paper{Vision-Language Models for Zero-Shot Weed Detection and Visual Reasoning in UAV-Based Precision Agriculture}
{M. F. Nasir, M. U. Rehman, I. Hussain}
{Frontiers in Plant Science, vol.~16, Art.~no.~1735096, 2026}
{10.3389/fpls.2025.1735096}
\pp{Introduction} Deep weed detectors need annotation, generalise poorly and explain little; can
commercial VLMs do better zero-shot?~\cite{nasir2026vlmweed}
\pp{Proposed solution} Six commercial VLMs (ChatGPT-4.1, ChatGPT-4o, Gemini Flash~2.5, Gemini Flash
Lite~2.5, LLaMA-4 Scout and Maverick) are given soybean drone images with one prompt asking for weed
presence, location, reasoning, growth stage and crop, plus a counterfactual follow-up prompt.
\pp{Results} Gemini Flash~2.5 was the most consistent; weed detection accuracy ranged from 0.15
(LLaMA-4 Scout) to 0.75 (Gemini Flash~2.5). Interpretability tracked spatial correctness.
\pp{Gap / limitation} Even the best model is wrong often enough that it cannot decide alone.
\pp{Relevance} Motivates our design rule that a VLM advises but never decides what is sprayed
(Section~\ref{sec:agent}).

% ─────────────────────────────── Background works
\section{Background works}\label{sec:lit-background}
\textbf{Image processing and deep learning in agriculture.} Hamuda, Glavin and
Jones~\cite{hamuda2016survey} surveyed plant extraction in the field, where colour indices followed
by an automatic threshold such as Otsu's are a standard first step. Tian et al.~\cite{tian2020cv}
reviewed computer vision for agricultural automation and called for large-scale datasets and
robustness in complex environments. Kamilaris and Prenafeta-Bold\'u~\cite{kamilaris2018dl} surveyed 40
studies of deep learning in agriculture and found that it generally outperformed earlier image
processing. Hasan et al.~\cite{hasan2021survey} surveyed deep learning for weed detection by data,
detection approach and learning method, and Lu and Young~\cite{lu2020datasets} reviewed public
field datasets (15 for weed control, 10 for fruit detection, 9 others). Dos Santos Ferreira et
al.~\cite{dossantos2017soybean} classified superpixels of soybean drone images with a ConvNet.

\textbf{Segmentation and detection networks.} Fully convolutional networks~\cite{long2017fcn} made
pixel-wise prediction end to end; SegNet~\cite{badrinarayanan2017segnet} reuses max-pooling indices
in its decoder; DeepLab~\cite{chen2018deeplab} adds atrous convolution, spatial pyramid pooling and a
CRF; ERFNet~\cite{romera2018erfnet} uses factorised residual layers for real-time segmentation and is
WaW's supervised baseline; Faster R-CNN~\cite{ren2017fasterrcnn} introduced the region proposal
network. SAM~2~\cite{ravi2025sam2} extends SAM to video and is the segmenter we use. Singh et
al.~\cite{singh2025fewshot} found that few-shot adaptation of Grounding DINO works better than
zero-shot prompts on agricultural classes.

\textbf{Vision-language models, LLMs and agents.} Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2024vlmsurvey} surveyed
VLMs for visual recognition: architectures, pre-training objectives, transfer and zero-shot use.
Yin et al.~\cite{yin2025foundation} reviewed agricultural foundation models and their challenges
(data acquisition, training efficiency, data shift). Kuska, Wahabzada and Paulus~\cite{kuska2024llm}
mapped where LLMs may help in crop production, and Li et al.~\cite{li2025llmagri} discussed
grounding LLMs in agricultural knowledge bases; AgriAgent takes every herbicide dose from the ICAR
bulletin~\cite{choudhury2016icar} for this reason. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2024agents} surveyed
LLM-based autonomous agents; our escalation agent is a deliberately small instance with fixed tools,
a call budget and no authority to spray.
